Looking for a Pocket Alternative? Here's What Actually Matters in 2026

Pocket's design wasn't sacred. The real question is what you were actually doing with it — because that determines which replacement will actually stick.

Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025. If you exported your data before the window closed that November, you're fine. If you didn't, it's gone — years of saved articles, tags, and reading history, deleted along with the service.

A lot of people spent last year quietly rebuilding their reading workflow from scratch. If you're one of them — or if you're just now getting around to it — the real question isn't "which app looks like Pocket." Pocket's design wasn't sacred. The question is what you actually used it for, because that's what determines which replacement will actually stick.

Start with what you were really doing

Most people used Pocket for some mix of these, and they don't all point to the same replacement:

  • Quick capture — one click, done, sort it out later
  • Actually reading it later — which, if we're honest, most saved articles never got
  • Offline reading — on a flight, a commute, in bed with the phone on airplane mode
  • Search — finding that one article again, three months later, remembering one phrase from it
  • Not losing it all again — this one's new, and it's the one Pocket users didn't used to think about

If you were mostly doing the first one, almost any bookmarking tool works. It's the other four where the real differences show up.

The landscape, honestly

Instapaper is the closest thing to a like-for-like Pocket replacement — clean reader view, Kindle delivery baked in, been doing this since 2008. If Kindle delivery was the feature you used most, it's a safe, boring, well-tested choice.

Raindrop.io is the strongest free option if you're mostly bookmarking links rather than reading long-form text in-app — collections and organization over reading experience.

Readwise Reader is built for people who highlight heavily and want that data to go somewhere. Great tool, but it's solving a different problem than "I saved this, now what."

Wallabag and other self-hosted options are the right call if data ownership is the whole point for you — you run the server, nobody can shut it down but you.

AI Summary Helper — this one, to be direct about it — starts from a different assumption: that the bottleneck isn't saving articles, it's that saved piles turn into guilt piles. So instead of just being a nicer storage bin, it tries to close the loop: AI-generated summaries so you know what you saved without re-reading it cold, a search that actually indexes full article text, and a Digest feature that picks a handful of your saves — deliberately varied, not five versions of the same story — and bundles them into one file to send to Kindle or a device on your network.

It's Chrome-native rather than a separate app, and most of what it does beyond the AI summary itself — search, duplicate detection, tag cleanup, recommendations — runs entirely on your device. No separate account required to get real value, and if you want zero cloud dependency at all, it works with a local Ollama model instead of a hosted AI provider.

A quick decision guide

  • Want the safest, most proven Kindle-delivery workflow? Instapaper.
  • Mostly bookmarking links, not reading long articles in-app? Raindrop.io.
  • Heavy highlighter, want spaced repetition on what you read? Readwise Reader.
  • Non-negotiable about owning your own data? Wallabag, self-hosted.
  • Want AI summaries, on-device search, and a tool that actively tries to get you to actually read what you saved? That's the gap AI Summary Helper is built for.

None of these are wrong choices. The mistake, if there is one, is picking based on which name sounds most like Pocket instead of which habit you're actually trying to rebuild.
