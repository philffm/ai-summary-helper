Digital Sovereignty for Individuals: What It Means and What You Can Actually Do

Most writing on digital sovereignty is about governments and companies. This one is about you: your saved articles, your notes, your AI tools, and who can switch them off.

The short answer. Digital sovereignty, for a person, means being able to say where your data lives, who can read it, and what happens to it if a company changes its prices, its terms or its mind. It does not mean doing everything yourself. It means knowing your dependencies and choosing them on purpose.

What is digital sovereignty, in plain words?

Governments use the term for control over their own infrastructure and data. For individuals it breaks down into three questions you can actually answer:

  • Where is it? Which company, which country, which device holds your notes, your reading list, your photos.
  • Who can read it? The provider, its staff, an authority that can compel the provider, or only you.
  • Can you leave? Can you take everything out, in a format other software opens, on the day you decide to?

If you can answer all three for the things you care about, you are sovereign over them. For most people the honest answer today is "no idea" for most of them. That is fixable, and not all at once.

Why it matters now: five things that changed

1. A European data centre does not make a US company European. The US CLOUD Act lets US authorities require US-based providers to hand over data they control, wherever it is stored. How that fits with the GDPR is disputed: European bar associations have argued that handing over EU personal data on such an order would breach it. Lawyers explain the conflict in detail (Linklaters, Cleary Gottlieb, CCBE assessment). The practical lesson: "hosted in the EU" tells you where the server stands, not whose law reaches it.

2. The legal bridge for data sent to the US is under appeal. Transfers of personal data to certified US companies currently rest on the EU-US Data Privacy Framework. Its two predecessors, Safe Harbor and Privacy Shield, were both struck down by the EU's highest court. In September 2025 the EU General Court dismissed a challenge to the new framework; the claimant appealed, and the appeal (case C-703/25 P) is still pending at the time of writing (WilmerHale, Digital Policy Alert). The framework is valid today. Whether it stays valid is not something an individual can control, which is the point.

3. Leaving a cloud service is becoming a legal right. The EU Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025. Cloud providers must let customers switch with at most two months' notice, may only charge their own cost for switching until January 2027, and from 12 January 2027 may not charge switching fees at all (Latham & Watkins, McCann FitzGerald). These rules are aimed at cloud and infrastructure customers, not at your reading app, but they show where the law is heading: portability is a baseline, not a favour.

4. Services disappear. Mozilla announced in May 2025 that it would shut Pocket down on 8 July 2025, with an export-only period afterwards that was later extended to 6 November 2025 (9to5Mac, Vice). Everyone who had not exported by then lost their saved articles. We wrote about what to look for in a replacement in the Pocket alternative guide.

5. Public bodies are moving, and showing it can be done. The German state of Schleswig-Holstein has been replacing Microsoft software in its administration with open-source tools under a programme called "Digital Souveräner Arbeitsplatz", starting with mail and office software; Mecklenburg-Vorpommern has agreed to cooperate with it (reports: PC-WELT, CIO). It has been slow and not without criticism, and they are not dropping Microsoft entirely. If a state administration can change course step by step, you can do it with a reading list.

Sovereignty is a ladder, not a switch

"Sovereign or not" is the wrong question. A better one is: how far up the ladder do you want to be, for which kind of data?

LevelWhat it looks likeYou are protected against
0 · DefaultEverything in one provider's cloud, no export triedNothing: the provider decides
1 · Exit planYou have exported once and know the format opens elsewhereA shutdown (the Pocket case)
2 · Your own accountsYour own API key, your own storage account, no bundled middlemanPrice and terms changes of a bundled service
3 · Your own storageData on a server or drive you control, in open formatsOne provider holding the only copy
4 · Encrypted, keys with youWhat leaves your device is encrypted; only you hold the keyThe storage provider, and anyone who can compel it
5 · Local computeModels and tools run on your own machineThe AI provider seeing your content

Almost nobody is at level 5 for everything, and nobody needs to be. A reading list and a private journal do not need the same level. The useful habit is knowing which level each thing is at.

Local-first: the engineering idea behind owning your data

In 2019 researchers at the lab Ink & Switch published an essay, "Local-first software", that gave this a name. Their seven ideals include that work should not wait on a server, that the network is optional, that your data should still be usable "decades" later even if the company behind the software is gone, that security and privacy are the default (any server holding copies stores only encrypted data it cannot read), and that you keep ultimate ownership and control (Ink & Switch, 2019). It is a useful checklist for any tool you depend on: ask which of the seven it breaks.

A checklist for one weekend

  1. List your dependencies. For notes, reading list, passwords, photos and AI tools: which company, which country, which format.
  2. Test one export. Not "can I export" but "I exported and opened the file in a second app". Do it for the thing you would miss most.
  3. Prefer open formats. Markdown, plain text, JSON, EPUB, standard calendar and contact files. They outlive apps.
  4. Pick your own storage. A Nextcloud server (self-hosted or from a European provider), an S3-compatible bucket you own the account for, or just a folder that Syncthing or your desktop sync client already carries between devices.
  5. Encrypt before it leaves. The question to ask any sync or backup: who holds the key? If the answer is "the provider", the provider can read it.
  6. Choose how your AI is reached. Your own API key (you pay per use and can switch provider in a minute), a model on your own computer, or a bundled subscription. Each is a level on the ladder.
  7. Keep a backup you have restored. A backup you never restored is a hope. Restore it once, on another device.

How AI Summary Helper handles this, and where it does not

We build AI Summary Helper, a browser extension that summarizes pages, PDFs and videos and keeps a reading library. We try to make the ladder climbable inside one tool. Here is what that means in practice, including the limits.

  • Your library stays on your device. Articles, summaries, highlights and notes are stored locally in your browser. There is no account for the core features (privacy policy).
  • You choose how the AI is reached. Bring your own key for a provider you pick (why that matters), run a local model with Ollama so page content never leaves your machine, or use our optional hosted service. With a local model, the summary itself needs no network at all.
  • Sync and backup on your own storage. Add a Nextcloud or other WebDAV server, an S3-compatible bucket or a folder on your computer (step by step). In encrypted places everything is encrypted in your browser (AES-256-GCM, a key derived from your passphrase) before it leaves the device, and one passphrase is all you keep. The storage provider sees scrambled files.
  • Publish on your own site. A readable place writes plain files and an optional viewer page for your own website, only for the articles you tag. Notes and full texts stay out unless you switch them on.
  • An EU option for the hosted service. If you use our optional hosted service, a "Prefer EU servers" switch in the settings sends your requests only to EU endpoints of the model provider, never to a fallback elsewhere. Models are marked EU company (Mistral, which is based in Paris) or EU servers (a US company's EU region), because those are not the same thing. Models that have no EU route stay selectable but are marked, and the Auto choice never picks them while the switch is on.
  • Your AI apps, on your computer. Optionally, desktop AI apps can read your library through a local connection, with nothing going through our servers (how that works).
  • Open source. The code is public on GitHub, so what we claim here can be checked.

What it does not do. Being honest matters more than a clean story:

  • It runs inside a browser made by a US company. A local model removes the AI provider from the picture; it does not remove the browser.
  • Most hosted AI providers are US companies. If you use one with your own key, the page text you summarize goes to them. Only a local model keeps it entirely on your machine.
  • Our optional hosted service runs its server and its database at IONOS in Germany. Your text still goes on to a model provider through OpenRouter, a US company. The EU switch pins the model's servers to the EU, it does not change who the companies are. Treat the hosted service as a convenience with an EU option, not as the sovereign choice: for that, use your own key or a local model.
  • The encryption key for a sync place is kept in your browser profile so syncing can run in the background. Someone with access to your unlocked profile could use it. The storage provider still sees how many files there are, their sizes and when they change.
  • The encryption design is open but has not been independently audited.
  • A readable (published) place is not encrypted. That is its purpose, and the settings screen says so.

Questions people ask

Is storing my data in the EU enough?

It helps with latency and some legal questions, but it does not settle who can compel access: that depends on the company's jurisdiction, not only the server's location. Encryption with keys you hold changes the picture more than a postcode does.

Is a European AI model the same as running on EU servers?

No. A company based in Europe, such as Mistral in Paris, is subject to EU and national law. A US company's model running in an EU region is physically in Europe, but the company behind it is still American, which is the CLOUD Act point above. Both can be reasonable choices, and neither is a guarantee. That is why we label them separately. Check the provider's current terms and hosting, because partnerships and investors change.

Do I have to self-host to be sovereign?

No. Self-hosting is one way to reach levels 3 and 4. A European provider, or a bucket under your own account with encryption on your side, gets you much of the way with far less work.

Are local AI models good enough to summarize articles?

For summaries of ordinary articles, mid-sized open models on a recent laptop are usable. Quality and speed depend on your hardware and the model. The honest approach is to try one on your own reading before deciding.

Is encryption enough on its own?

No. It protects content from the storage provider, but not from a weak passphrase, a compromised device, or metadata such as file sizes and timing. A random multi-word passphrase kept in a password manager is the practical minimum.

What if the tool I use disappears?

That is what the exit test is for. If your data sits in open formats on storage you control, the worst case is changing the app, not losing the data.
